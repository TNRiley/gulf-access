Florida Gulf coast · residential canals · 1 m lidar

Gulf Access

Cape Coral says it has 300 miles of freshwater canal. One-metre lidar finds miles of canal in the city, and only miles of it stands above the height of the tide. The state's own waterbody map counts miles as fresh. Neither instrument can find the other half of the city's figure.

The canals here are not taken from any map. They are read off the ground: ten-kilometre tiles of the USGS lidar surface across 22 Gulf-coast counties, searched for narrow water with houses on it, then sorted by the one thing a laser measures well, which is how high the water stands.

01 · the claim

Three counts of one city's fresh water

Cape Coral is the largest canal city on the coast and the only one that publishes a split. Its utilities department states that the “freshwater canal system is comprised of 300 miles of freshwater canals”, and the city describes the whole system as 400 miles. Here is that figure beside two measurements that do not depend on it or on each other.

Miles of canal in Cape Coral, by who is counting

Orange is fresh water held above the tide. Blue is everything else each source counts.

The lidar measurement is a floor and a ceiling at once. It finds km of canal inside the city limits ( miles, or % of the 400 the city claims in total, so very little is going unseen). Of that, km has its water surface more than 0.6 m above sea level, higher than an ordinary tide stands here, so that water is certainly held. The line sits where the city's water sorts itself: only km of canal stands between 0.55 and 1.05 m, with km below the gap and the weir staircase above it.

The second measure is the Florida Department of Environmental Protection's waterbody map, which draws Cape Coral as separate assessment units and classes each as stream (fresh) or estuary. It puts km of the same canals in fresh units.

The two agree on % of the city's canal length: km both call held and fresh, km both put at the tide. They part on km that the state calls fresh but that stands inside the tide's range, which is exactly where a laser cannot referee.

For the city's 300 miles to be right, nearly every canal standing between 0.1 and 0.5 m would have to be fresh water held a hand's breadth above the sea, including the stretches the state classes as estuary. The city does not say how it counts. It may be counting shoreline, or lakes, or something this page cannot see. What can be said is that the figure cannot be recovered from the ground or from the state's map.

03 · the coast

What counts as a canal moves the answer

The detector finds km of narrow water of every kind in the tiles. Keeping only what runs past residential streets leaves km, and that still mixes dredged canals with subdivision lakes. There is no clean geometric line between the two, so the rule is yours to change. Under every rule here Cape Coral's held canal comes to between and miles, nowhere near 300. The coast-wide share at tide height is another matter.

04 · the lots

The lots stand a metre above the marsh

A canal estate is made by digging the canal and piling what comes out onto the lots. The lidar cannot tell fill from ground that was already high, but it can measure the step. Along tidal canals the ground within 45 m of the water stands a median m above it. Along the unbuilt tidal creeks in the same tiles, with no residential street near them, the banks stand m.

How far the ground stands above the water

Length-weighted. The bar spans the middle half of canal length, the whiskers the middle 80%, the tick is the median. Two independent readings for the canals: the lots beside the water, and the crown of the nearest residential street.

The street reading runs about a quarter of a metre under the lot reading in both classes, as it should: houses sit on pads above their roads. The two were never combined. They agree on the comparison that matters, which is that held canals keep roughly the same freeboard ( m by lots, m by streets) as tidal ones ( m and m), from sea level to water held m up.

The unbuilt creeks are the control. If the detector's idea of a bank simply produced a metre and a half everywhere, the creeks would show it too. They do not. Water level and bank height track each other tightly on every kind of water, built or not, so that correlation is not offered here as a finding: it would be true of a puddle.

05 · the instruments

Sea level is a number somebody typed

Three things about the tools turned out to matter as much as anything about the canals.

The sea stands at exactly 0.00 m

Level of open water at least 300 m across, one reading per tile, for the tiles that have any. In of them it reads 0.00 m. That is not the tide. Lidar contractors flatten tidal water to a constant, and most of this coast got zero. Canals inside the towns were flattened one at a time to whatever stage they were at, which is why tidal canals read anywhere from 0.00 to about 0.5 m.

The map does not have most of them

Share of each town's detected canal length that lies within 40 m of an OpenStreetMap waterway=canal line. Tidal finger canals are usually drawn as coastline instead, with no centreline at all.

The third: water under about 30 m wide is not always flattened at all. A detector that asks for perfectly flat water finds 141 km of canal in one test tile of Cape Coral; the same tile holds about 225 km. The narrow canals had to be picked up as trenches standing at the level of the flat water nearby.

06 · methods

What was measured, and what was not

Sources

  • USGS 3D Elevation Program, 1 m lidar DEM, read through The National Map's 3DEPElevation image service (exportImage, 32-bit float, nearest neighbour, 5 m pixels in UTM 17N), 512 tiles of 10 km. US Government work, public domain.
  • OpenStreetMap, Florida extract from Geofabrik dated 2026-11-10: residential streets (to decide what is residential), river and stream lines (to set aside natural water), canal lines and coastline (for comparison only). © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL 1.0.
  • Florida DEP Waterbody IDs (WBIDs), 6,894 assessment polygons with a water type each, from the department's open data service. Florida public record, credited to DEP's Division of Environmental Assessment and Restoration.
  • US Census TIGERweb county and place boundaries, to name where each canal is. Public domain.
  • City of Cape Coral, Utilities, Water Reclamation page, read 2026-11-11, for the quoted 300 miles; and the city's water quality page for “400-mile canal system”.

How a canal is found

  • Water is any 15 m window of the surface that is flat to 3 cm, grown out over neighbours at the same height, plus trenches at least 1.2 m deep that stand within 0.3 m of the level of flat water inside 800 m. Lidar contractors flatten water bodies; bare ground is never that flat.
  • Narrow water is the centreline of that mask wherever the channel is 7.5 to 110 m wide. Length is measured along the centreline.
  • A piece is a connected run of centreline. It is kept if it is at least 150 m long, at least six times longer than wide, and cut at least 0.3 m below the ground beside it.
  • Residential: at least half its length lies within 120 m of an OpenStreetMap residential street. Pieces OpenStreetMap labels river or stream for half their length are set aside.
  • Canal, by the default rule: a residential piece that belongs to a network at least 2 km long, or whose banks run ruler-straight for half its length (orientation coherence of the water's edge of 0.75 or more in a 30 m window).
  • Level is the low median of the surface inside the channel, in metres NAVD88. Held means above 0.6 m. At tide height means 0.6 m or below.

Controls, and what they showed

  • Length. The city's 400 miles for Cape Coral was the check on the detector. It finds miles of residential narrow water there, under the canal rule. Had it found half, nothing else on this page would have been reported.
  • Two classifications, never merged. Lidar level and the state's water type agree on % of canal length coast-wide. Where they disagree the page shows the disagreement in violet instead of choosing.
  • Unbuilt creeks as the control for the fill measurement, described above.
  • Canal towns and lake suburbs as the control for the canal rule, with its error rates printed beside the rule.

What this does not say

  • It does not count saltwater canals. Rotonda West's canals are fresh and held at 0.2 to 0.45 m, inside the tide's range; Apollo Beach's are plainly tidal and the state's polygon there says stream. Below 0.6 m the lidar cannot tell held from tidal, and the state's map is wrong in places. Only “held above 0.6 m” is certain.
  • It does not say the city is wrong, only that its figure cannot be reproduced. The city does not publish its method.
  • Nothing here is about whether a boat can reach the Gulf. Bridges, locks, depth and distance are not measured.
  • Levels are the stage on the day each area was flown, and tidal water is mostly a contractor's constant, not a tide.
  • Water wider than 110 m is left out, so lakes and basins add nothing to any length. Canals under about 15 m wide, and ones roofed by trees or culverted, are missed.
  • The metre between canal lots and unbuilt creek banks is not all fill. Some estates were cut into pine flatwoods that already stood above the marsh.
  • Freeboard is not flood risk. It is the height of lots and streets above the canal on a calm day.
  • Monroe County and the Keys are not included. Tiles with under about 9 km of residential street were not downloaded.

What failed

  • The map as the map. The first plan took canal centrelines from OpenStreetMap. It has 24 km of them on Marco Island, where the lidar finds km, and none in Apollo Beach. The national hydrography dataset draws those same canals as coastline. The plan was dropped and the lidar became the map.
  • Trenches. Looking for anything cut below the house lots found every street in Cape Coral: 953 km of “canal” in one tile. Lots are filled above the roads too.
  • The ruler. Straightness was meant to separate dug canals from natural and landscaped water everywhere. It recognises grid canals (94% of Cape Coral's length) and fails on finger canals: 6% in Punta Gorda, 1% in Hernando Beach. It survives only as half of an either-or rule.
  • Level as a tidal test. The first classifier called everything at or below 0.6 m tidal. Rotonda West broke it.
  • A coast-wide tidal share. It moves by more than twenty points with the definition of a canal, so it is shown as a range and not as a result.
